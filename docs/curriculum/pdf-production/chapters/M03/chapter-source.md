## 3. Read the road before acting

Reading a road means combining instructions, movement, visibility and uncertainty. A sign tells you something important; it rarely tells you everything needed for the next decision.

### M03-L01 · Recognise what a sign asks

**Your goal:** Classify the main sign families and describe the decision each one prompts.

Start with purpose, then read the specific symbol and any supplementary panel. The official catalogue groups signs by function. Do not rely on colour alone: similar shapes can communicate different duties, and some important signs have distinctive shapes. [Transportstyrelsen: sign catalogue][signs]

| Family | What to establish | A useful driving question |
|---|---|---|
| A — warning | The nature of a hazard ahead | What might be hidden, and how much space do I need? |
| B — priority | A duty or relationship at a conflict point | Must I yield or stop, and to whom? |
| C — prohibition/restriction | What movement, vehicle or limit is restricted | Does this apply to my vehicle and journey? |
| D — mandatory instruction | The required route, side or permitted traffic | Which path must I take? |
| E — instruction | A road or area with a particular meaning or regime | Which rules begin or end here? |

These are learning questions, not substitutes for the individual sign descriptions. A warning of children, for example, does not assign a new numerical speed by itself; your speed must still allow safe control and a response to foreseeable hazards. A posted maximum speed is a ceiling, not a demand to maintain that speed. [Road Signs Regulation, chapter 2][sign-law] · [Traffic Regulation, chapter 3 §§14–15][traffic-law]

**Requirements.** B1 means yield to vehicles on the crossing road or route. Make that intention clear early and proceed only without danger or obstruction. B2 requires a stop before entering the crossing road or track area. Stop at the stop line; without one, stop immediately before the signal or before entering the crossing road or track area, as applicable. A rolling pause does not meet a stop duty. [Road Signs Regulation, B1–B2][sign-law] · [Traffic Regulation, chapter 3 §§5 and 19][traffic-law]

**Worked decision.** You see a warning sign for a narrow section. You reduce speed and look for oncoming traffic before reaching it. You then read any priority instruction at the narrow point. The warning alone did not decide who should pass first.

**Knowledge check.** Does seeing a warning sign tell you an exact safe speed? **Answer:** No. Assess the hazard, visibility, road surface and people present as well as the applicable limit.

**Remember:** Identify the instruction and turn it into an early action. The illustrated sign reference is prepared separately from this text.

### M03-L02 · Combine the instructions

**Your goal:** Interpret signs, panels, markings and signals together, including authorised directions.

**Requirements.** Follow the traffic instructions that apply to you. Where an instruction differs from a general traffic rule, the instruction governs. A steady traffic-light indication takes precedence over a yield or stop sign; a police officer's direction takes precedence over the rules and ordinary traffic instructions. This is not a rule that a green signal cancels every other restriction. Authorised guards also give traffic directions that must be followed. [Traffic Regulation, chapter 2 §§2–4][traffic-law]

For ordinary vehicle signals: red means stop; red and amber together still mean stop, with green approaching. Green permits movement, subject to other duties and space ahead. Amber means stop unless, when green changes to amber, you are already so close that stopping would be dangerous. Flashing amber requires particular caution; read the signs and applicable rules. Arrow signals apply to the direction indicated. [Road Signs Regulation, chapter 3 §§3–6][sign-law]

A red lane-control cross closes the lane; a green downward arrow allows its use; a diagonal amber arrow tells you to move in the indicated direction. Make any lane change safely. Signals for pedestrians or public transport are not automatically instructions for your car. [Road Signs Regulation, chapter 3 §§7–15][sign-law]

**Markings.** A broken centre or lane line identifies a boundary; it does not promise that overtaking is safe. A warning line indicates that crossing is unsuitable because of conditions and may precede a continuous line. Continuous lines and hatched areas have crossing restrictions, with specified exceptions such as a necessary passage around an obstruction under particular caution. A painted line is not an invitation to improvise an exception. [Road Signs Regulation, chapter 4, M1/M3/M8/M9][sign-law] · [Traffic Regulation, chapter 3 §§9–11][traffic-law]

Supplementary panels qualify the sign above them: read the distance, length, direction, time or vehicle symbol as part of the instruction. A distance **to** a hazard and the **length** of a hazardous stretch answer different questions. [Supplementary panels][panels]

**Worked decision.** Your signal is green, but a queue fills the space beyond the junction. Wait where you will not obstruct crossing traffic rather than entering a space you cannot clear. [Traffic Regulation, chapter 3 §20][traffic-law]

**Knowledge check.** Does amber mean accelerate to beat red? **Answer:** No. The rule is to stop; the exception concerns an unsafe stop after the change, not a wish to save time.

**Remember:** Read the whole instruction and the space beyond it.

### M03-L03 · Find your way without rushing

**Your goal:** Use direction, facility and service signs to prepare route choices early.

Direction information appears before and at decision points. An advance direction sign helps you understand a junction or exit before reaching it; lane direction information helps you select a suitable lane. Direction signs for walking and cycling serve those modes and should not be mistaken for a car route. Facility, service and tourism signs help locate destinations; they do not cancel restrictions on access. [Road Signs Regulation, chapter 2, F/G/H/I families][sign-law]

**Learning approach.** Know your next destination or route number, look for advance information and compare it with the road layout. Check the lane and movement required, then establish a safe opportunity to position the car. This is easier when navigation is prepared while parked and passengers understand that late instructions may be declined.

A symbol or route number may be easier to recognise than a place name. Learn the relevant symbols from the official catalogue rather than inventing an interpretation. Information signs can describe a changed route or other conditions; read enough of the message early to decide safely. [Transportstyrelsen: sign catalogue][signs]

**Requirement.** Destination signs can permit using the lane suited to your onward route, but changing into it still has to be safe. Do not cross a continuous lane boundary merely because you have chosen the wrong exit. [Traffic Regulation, chapter 3 §§7, 9 and 43][traffic-law]

**Worked decision.** A navigation instruction arrives after a driver has passed the safe lane-change opportunity. The driver continues in the permitted direction and finds another route. A missed turn is a planning problem; a sudden crossing of lanes creates a traffic problem.

**Knowledge check.** A service sign shows fuel at a junction. Does that guarantee that every approach or vehicle may enter the access road? **Answer:** No. Follow the access signs and restrictions as well as the destination information.

**Remember:** A route can be corrected later. An unsafe last-second manoeuvre cannot be planned away afterwards.

### M03-L04 · Leave room to respond

**Your goal:** Explain how observation, speed, reaction, braking, grip and following space affect your options.

**Requirements.** Choose a speed that preserves control and lets you stop within the road you can see and before a foreseeable obstruction. Your distance from the vehicle ahead must allow for it slowing or stopping. Weather, visibility, road condition, vehicle condition and loading all matter. [Traffic Regulation, chapter 3 §§2 and 14–16][traffic-law]

For an ordinary car, the general speed limits are 50 km/h within a built-up area and 70 km/h outside it, unless another applicable limit has been prescribed. The motorway default is 110 km/h; 120 km/h can be prescribed. Local and road-specific limits often differ, and a lower vehicle-combination limit still applies. Read the current signs and conditions rather than selecting a speed from the road’s appearance. [Traffic Regulation, chapter 3 §17, chapter 4 §20 and chapter 9 §1][traffic-law]

Think of stopping in two parts. During the time between noticing a hazard and beginning effective braking, the vehicle still travels. After that, it travels while slowing. More speed consumes more distance during the same delay and increases the energy that must be removed. Less available friction reduces what the tyres can do for braking and steering. [Trafikverket: speed and impact][impact] · [VTI friction report, sections 4.2–4.3][friction]

**Illustrative calculation, not a stopping-distance prediction.** A speed of 50 km/h is approximately 13.9 metres per second: 50 ÷ 3.6. If a hypothetical response delay is one second, that is about 14 metres before braking begins. At 80 km/h the same assumed delay consumes about 22 metres. These figures are unit conversions using an assumed delay; people do not all react in one second, and braking distance has not yet been added.

**Learning approach.** Scan far enough ahead to see developing situations, while revisiting the near area, mirrors and places where someone may enter. Avoid staring at one hazard until everything else disappears from attention. Increase your margin when information is poor. A timed following-gap check can help you notice a short gap, but no fixed number is safe in every condition.

**Safety advice · Check a timed gap.** For the three-second following-distance guideline, select a fixed roadside point, such as a pole or sign. When the rear of the vehicle ahead passes it, start counting at a steady seconds pace. Stop when the front of your car reaches the same point. Arriving before three seconds indicates a short gap against that guideline: ease back smoothly and check again. Three seconds is a guideline, not a statutory fixed interval or a guarantee; poor grip, visibility or other hazards require more space and an appropriate speed. Keep observing the road while counting. [NSW Government: fixed-point following-gap method][following-gap]

**Fixed-point example.** The car ahead passes a sign. You count “one thousand and one, one thousand and two, one thousand and three”. If your car reaches the sign while you are still on “two”, increase the gap smoothly. At a hypothetical constant 60 km/h, three seconds represents 60 ÷ 3.6 × 3 = 50 metres. A timed gap therefore represents more metres at higher speed, without requiring you to estimate metres by eye.

**Safety advice · When someone follows too closely.** Increase your gap to the vehicle ahead gradually, allowing you to ease off and brake more gently if traffic slows. This gives the following driver more time to respond. Do not brake sharply to warn or punish them, or accelerate into an unsafe speed. If an emergency requires braking, brake as necessary; this advice does not mean withholding it. [Northern Ireland government: tailgater response][vehicle-faults]

**Tailgater example.** Maya has a close follower and sees brake lights farther ahead. She eases off early to create room in front and slows progressively. She cannot control the follower, but she can reduce the need for a sudden stop.

**Physical explanation · Twice the speed.** With the same effective grip and braking deceleration on the same gradient, braking distance is approximately proportional to speed squared: **2 × speed → 4 × braking distance**. In a constant-deceleration model, distance = speed² ÷ (2 × deceleration), with speed in metres per second. Doubling speed multiplies the numerator by four. Real conditions and brake response can change; this is a comparison under stated assumptions. [Trafikverket: VGU braking-distance equation][braking-model]

**Hypothetical comparison.** Assume a level road, unchanged vehicle/load/grip, constant braking deceleration of 5 m/s² and a one-second response delay. At 40 km/h, braking distance is about 12.3 m and reaction distance 11.1 m. At 80 km/h they are 49.4 m and 22.2 m: braking quadruples while reaction distance doubles. Total stopping distances are about 23.5 m and 71.6 m, so the total does **not** quadruple. These invented model conditions explain the relationship; they are not predicted stopping distances for your car.

**Approximate exercise model · Keep the methods separate.** Define v as speed in km/h and t as the supplied reaction time in seconds. Exact constant-speed reaction distance is (v ÷ 3.6) × t metres; braking is not included. When an exercise specifies the rough method below, use both rough estimates together rather than substituting an exact reaction value.

**Rough reaction estimate:** R = (v ÷ 10) × t × 3 metres. Divide speed by ten, multiply by the supplied seconds, then by three. [Trafiko: approximate stopping method][stopping-shortcut] documents the one-second shortcut; multiplication by a supplied t is editorial proportional arithmetic. Half a second gives half the one-second estimate; two seconds gives twice it. These are supplied exercise inputs, not claims about every driver's reaction time.

**Rough braking estimate:** B = (v ÷ 10)² × 0.4 metres. Divide speed by ten, square that result, then multiply by 0.4. [Trafiko: approximate stopping method][stopping-shortcut] describes this shortcut under good-brake, good-tyre and dry-asphalt assumptions. The coefficient is an exercise assumption, not a universal result for a real car or road. Supplied reaction time does not change the braking part in this model.

**Rough total:** S = R + B. Calculate both parts separately with metres, then add. Keep decimal precision during the working; round only the final result if a precision is requested. These shortcuts teach arithmetic, not a safe-distance guarantee or a prediction for an actual hazard.

**Source and assumption limits.** Trafiko is a commercial teaching source, cited only for these approximate shortcuts. [Trafikverket: stopping-distance principles][stopping-principles] supports reaction plus braking and dependence on speed and conditions, but does not supply these constants. Its illustrated distances have different assumptions and must not be mixed with this model. Real stopping depends on grip, gradient, tyres, brakes, response and other conditions. Retain the exact conversion and separate constant-deceleration comparison above under their own assumptions.

**Worked approximate examples · Calculate both parts.** Use the rough exercise equations consistently; each reaction time below is a supplied hypothetical input.

**Example A · 40 km/h and 0.5 seconds.** First v ÷ 10 = 4. Reaction R = 4 × 0.5 × 3 = 6 m. Braking B = 4² × 0.4 = 6.4 m. Total S = 6 + 6.4 = **12.4 m**. The half-second input affects reaction, not braking.

**Example B · 70 km/h and one second.** First v ÷ 10 = 7. Reaction R = 7 × 1 × 3 = 21 m. Braking B = 7² × 0.4 = 19.6 m. Total S = 21 + 19.6 = **40.6 m**. Squaring seven gives 49; do not multiply speed itself by 0.4.

**Example C · 50 km/h and two seconds.** First v ÷ 10 = 5. Reaction R = 5 × 2 × 3 = 30 m. Braking B = 5² × 0.4 = 10 m. Total S = 30 + 10 = **40 m**. Doubling reaction time doubles this model's reaction estimate, leaving braking unchanged.

**Compare separately with exact conversion.** At 50 km/h and two seconds, exact constant-speed reaction distance is (50 ÷ 3.6) × 2 = about **27.8 m**, compared with the shortcut's 30 m. Dividing the shortcut factor 0.3 by the exact conversion factor 1 ÷ 3.6 gives 1.08: an **8% overestimate** for reaction distance. Mixing 27.8 m with rough braking of 10 m gives about 37.8 m, not the exercise model's 40 m. Keep exact conversion separate from the rough-model answer.

**Try the approximate process.** Calculate reaction, braking and total for (A) 30 km/h and 0.5 seconds, (B) 80 km/h and one second, and (C) 100 km/h and two seconds. Give each part in metres, check the square, then add. Answers are in the separate lesson answer section.

**Worked decision.** You can see over the roof of the car ahead that traffic is slowing. Ease your approach early rather than waiting for its brake lights at a short distance. The benefit is having choices before braking becomes urgent.

**Knowledge check.** (1) If response delay doubles at the same speed, what happens to reaction distance? (2) How do you time a three-second following gap? (3) Why increase the forward gap when someone follows too closely? (4) With unchanged braking deceleration, how does doubled speed affect braking distance, and does total stopping distance also quadruple? (5) Complete the three approximate exercises above and explain why you should keep exact reaction conversion separate. **Answer:** (1) It doubles under the constant-speed assumption. (2) Count seconds from the rear of the car ahead passing a fixed point until your front reaches that point; three seconds is a guideline and conditions may require more. (3) More forward space allows gentler slowing and gives the follower more time; avoid unnecessary sudden braking. (4) Braking distance becomes approximately four times as long, but reaction distance doubles for the same response delay, so the combined total does not generally quadruple. (5) A: reaction 4.5 m, braking 3.6 m, total 8.1 m. B: reaction 24 m, braking 25.6 m, total 49.6 m. C: reaction 60 m, braking 40 m, total 100 m. Use both rough equations together; substituting exact reaction conversion changes the exercise-model answer.

**Remember:** Space is useful because it gives you time to notice, decide and act.

### M03-R · Review and self-assessment

**Scenario:** You approach an unfamiliar junction with an advance direction sign, a supplementary panel, a changing signal and a queue beyond it.

Explain which information determines your route, which creates a duty, and where you would wait. **Review guide:** Select the relevant lane early, obey the applicable signal and panel, and keep the junction clear. A destination preference does not override a restriction.

Identify one type of information you noticed late. Explain the consequence and choose a reference or supervised observation exercise to improve it. [Syllabus self-assessment, chapters 2–3 §3][syllabus]
